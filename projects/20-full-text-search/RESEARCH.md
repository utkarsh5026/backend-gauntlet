
# Full-Text Search Engine Internals: A Systems Engineer's Deep Dive

## TL;DR

The state of the art in full-text retrieval is a mature core — an inverted index of immutable segments scored by BM25 with dynamic pruning (Block-Max WAND/MAXSCORE) — that in 2025–2026 is being reshaped by learned sparse retrieval (SPLADE) reusing that same inverted index, by hybrid dense+sparse fusion via Reciprocal Rank Fusion, and by the migration of segments onto object storage with stateless search (Quickwit, Elasticsearch stateless, Turbopuffer). For a from-scratch CPython builder, three things change your design most: (1) treat segments as strictly immutable, mmap'd, checksummed byte files and do all cleverness at merge time — lock-free reads, caching, and NRT refresh fall out for free; (2) BM25 needs no full sort and no full scan — a bounded min-heap plus optional block-max upper bounds is the whole game; and (3) the GIL, not your algorithm, is your latency ceiling for concurrent scoring — plan for free-threaded CPython 3.13/3.14 or process-per-shard from day one.

## Key Findings

- **BM25 defaults are `k1=1.2`, `b=0.75`** across Lucene, Elasticsearch and OpenSearch, and the IDF form is `log(1 + (N − df + 0.5)/(df + 0.5))` — the `+1` inside the log prevents the negative-IDF trap for terms in more than half the corpus.
- **The immutable-segment contract is the load-bearing invariant.** Readers never lock; a writer only appends a new segment; deletes are an overlay bitmap. NRT refresh, the request cache keyed to segment state, and lock-free scatter-gather are all downstream of this.
- **Store document length as a single quantized byte ("norm").** Lucene encodes field length via `SmallFloat.intToByte4` keeping only ~4 significant bits: many distinct lengths collapse to one of 256 values, guaranteeing *monotonicity only*, not exact length. Precompute a 256-entry `float[]` table so per-doc scoring is one array lookup.
- **A footer with a CRC32 is non-negotiable.** Lucene's `CodecUtil` writes `FOOTER_MAGIC` + algorithm ID (0 = zlib-crc32) + the checksum of all preceding bytes. Without it, a truncated or bit-flipped segment returns *wrong postings silently*.
- **Top-`k` is a bounded min-heap, never a sort.** Dynamic pruning (WAND, Block-Max WAND, MAXSCORE) stores a per-block maximum score so blocks of 128 postings skip without decoding. Per Elastic's own measurement (Adrien Grand/jpountz, "Faster Retrieval of Top Hits in Elasticsearch with Block-Max WAND"): recording impacts and implementing block-max WAND made **term queries between 3× and 7× faster, conjunctions between 3% and 7× faster, and disjunctions between −8% (slightly slower) and 15× faster** on Lucene's benchmark suite.
- **Compression is decode-speed vs ratio, and decode speed usually wins.** Delta+VByte is simple and fast; PForDelta/SIMD-BP128 hit **billions of ints/sec** but need SIMD you don't have in pure Python. At learning scale, delta + variable-byte on 128-doc blocks with skip pointers is the correct stop.
- **Refresh is the near-real-time knob and directly creates segments.** Elasticsearch's 1-second default refresh is why docs are searchable "in ~1s"; over-aggressive refresh (200–500ms) *raises* segment count and merge pressure and slows search. Refresh = visibility, flush/fsync = durability.
- **Tail latency is the enemy of fan-out.** Dean & Barroso, "The Tail at Scale" (CACM 2013): if one backend in 100 is slow, ~63% of fan-out requests hit a straggler. Their BigTable benchmark reads 1,000 keys across 100 servers; sending a hedging request after a 10ms delay reduced the 99.9th-percentile latency **from 1,800ms to 74ms while sending just 2% more requests**.
- **Per-shard IDF makes scores not strictly comparable across shards.** Query-then-fetch uses local stats; DFS-query-then-fetch does a preliminary round for *global* term stats. The skew matters most for rare terms on small shards.
- **The request cache must be invalidated on refresh.** Elasticsearch keys its shard request cache to exact segment state and drops it on every refresh — near-zero hit ratio on hot-ingest indices but over 90% on rarely-refreshed ones. Zipfian traffic makes 80%+ achievable.
- **The CPython GIL is your real ceiling.** Free-threaded 3.13 gave ~2.2× on 4 CPU-bound threads (~3.09× on 3.14), while the single-thread penalty dropped from ~40% on 3.13t to roughly 5–10% on 3.14t; the standard build serializes your scoring loop. Millions of small objects invite GC pauses — use `array`/`struct`/NumPy and `mmap`+`memoryview`.
- **BM25 is still the 2025 baseline that refuses to die.** Every learned-sparse and dense paper benchmarks against it; the frontier reuses the inverted index (SPLADE, uniCOIL) rather than replacing it.

## Details

### 1. Analysis: text → terms

**(a) Mechanism.** An analyzer is a pure function `str → list[term]`: char filter, tokenizer, token-filter chain. Naive `str.split()` fails on punctuation, CJK (no spaces), Unicode. The correct boundary rule is **Unicode UAX#29**. CJK needs dictionary segmentation or, cheaply, **CJK bigrams**; Thai needs dictionary segmentation. For autocomplete you build **edge-n-grams** at index time. Normalization has two axes: **Unicode form** (NFC composes; NFKC folds compatibility chars — more aggressive, occasionally destructive) and **case folding** (`str.casefold()` is Unicode-aware, handles German ß→"ss" which `.lower()` does not). The **iron invariant** is index-time/query-time symmetry: the identical analyzer must run on both paths, or "running" indexes as "run" and the query "running" never matches — the match silently vanishes with no error.

**(b) Production.** Lucene/Elasticsearch make the analyzer a property of the field mapping, used on both paths by default; a distinct `search_analyzer` is the one sanctioned asymmetry (edge-n-gram autocomplete). Classic bugs: changing the analyzer on a live index without reindexing; a synonym filter on only one side. Stop-words: Lucene/Elasticsearch **largely stopped removing them by default** — it broke phrase queries and hurt relevance; what replaced the optimization is **BM25 saturation plus dynamic pruning** (a term in 90% of docs has tiny IDF and its low-scoring blocks get skipped). Stemming (Porter — fast, aggressive, non-words like "argu") vs lemmatization (dictionary, correct, slower) vs decompounding (German). Exact fields (SKUs, emails) bypass the analyzer — Elasticsearch's `keyword` type. Changing an analyzer requires reindex: build new index, dual-write or reindex from source, atomically swap an **alias**.

**(c) Frontier.** Learned tokenization (WordPiece/BPE) blurs the boundary — SPLADE's "terms" are model-expanded. Borrow: UAX#29 segmentation, NFC + casefold, a toggleable stop-word set, optionally Porter.

### 2. The inverted index & on-disk format

**(a) Mechanism.** `term → sorted postings`, each `(doc_id, tf)` plus optional positions. Build a **term dictionary** (term → postings pointer) and the postings. At learning scale the simplest correct dictionary is a **sorted block dictionary**: terms in sorted UTF-8 order, grouped into blocks, binary-search the block index then scan within. **Front coding** stores `(shared_prefix_len, suffix)`: "search","searcher","searching" → "search","(6)er","(6)ing". Postings must be **sorted by doc_id** to enable delta encoding and skipping.

**(b) Production.** Lucene's term dictionary is a **BlockTree** combining an in-RAM **finite-state transducer (FST)** term index with on-disk term blocks; an FST maps a term to its file-pointer while sharing prefixes and suffixes (Mike McCandless: FSTs give a "sizable RAM reduction"). Lucene 10's BlockTree uses default block sizes 25–48 terms. Postings are in **blocks of 128 doc IDs and frequencies**, delta-encoded, FOR-delta packed or bit-set when dense; skip lists let a query jump without decoding. **Stored fields** (display) and **doc values** (columnar, sort/facet) are separate. Checksum: `CodecUtil.writeFooter` appends `FOOTER_MAGIC` + AlgorithmID (0 = zlib-crc32) + CRC32 of all preceding bytes; header carries `CODEC_MAGIC` + name + version + segment ID; a mismatch throws `CorruptIndexException`.

Compression families: **VByte** (7 bits + continuation, byte-aligned); **PForDelta** (bit width fitting most of a 128-block, rare exceptions patched separately — highest classic throughput); **SIMD-BP128/StreamVByte/Masked-VByte** (vectorized — Lemire & Boytsov, "Decoding billions of integers per second through vectorization," reports billions of 32-bit ints/sec; TurboPFor claims >8 GB/s); **Roaring bitmaps** for filters (2^16 chunks, each an array/bitmap/run container, always ≤8 KB to fit L1; the Roaring paper reports ~2× better compression and up to 900× faster intersections than WAH/Concise — used by Lucene, Spark, Druid). Tradeoff: SIMD wins on ratio and speed but needs vector intrinsics; **pure CPython has no SIMD** and per-integer Python decoding is catastrophic, so use delta + VByte over 128-doc blocks decoded through `memoryview`/NumPy.

**(c) Frontier.** SIMD/GPU postings decoding (GPUSparse, 2025–2026); Lucene vectorizes codecs via the Panama Vector API. Skip in CPython.

### 3. mmap, the page cache, and I/O

**(a) Mechanism.** `mmap` maps a file into virtual address space so reads become memory accesses served by the OS **page cache** — no per-access syscall, no kernel→user copy. A 10 GiB segment costs address space, not heap; pages fault in on touch, evict under pressure; hot terms stay cached free. Cost is **page-fault stalls** on cold access.

**(b) Production.** Lucene's `MMapDirectory` used Java `ByteBuffer`, capped at **2 GiB** per buffer (signed-32-bit limit), forcing awkward slicing and an `Unsafe` unmap hack. Lucene migrated to the **Panama `MemorySegment` API** (JEP 393/434): preview on JDK 19–21, fully supported on **JDK 22+** (PR #12706). It gives 64-bit addressing (Uwe Schindler chose a 16 GiB chunk so most segments map as one chunk and `max_map_count` rarely needs touching) and safe deterministic unmapping via `Arena`. `setPreload` forces pages into RAM; `MADV_RANDOM` disables sequential prefetch (right for random postings, wrong for a merge scan). Traps: **RSS vs virtual size** — `top` shows huge VIRT but RSS is what matters; measure via `/proc/self/status` before/after touching. NUMA and **huge pages** matter at scale. **io_uring/direct I/O** beats mmap on sequential scans and writes; mmap + page cache wins on random hot-term reads.

**Python specifics.** `mv = memoryview(mm)` then `mv[start:end]` is a **view, not a copy** — no bytes moved, no allocation. Trap: `bytes(mv[...])` **silently copies**. `struct.unpack_from(fmt, mm, offset)` reads in place. To *prove* no copy: measure RSS staying flat while scanning a multi-GiB file. **Biggest asyncio footgun**: `mmap` page faults and `open()/read()` are *blocking* — on the loop thread they stall every concurrent request. Detect with `PYTHONASYNCIODEBUG=1` and `loop.slow_callback_duration`; fix with `asyncio.to_thread` or keep hot segments resident.

**(c) Frontier.** Object-storage search does byte-range GETs against S3 with a small hotcache; Quickwit opens a split on S3 in ~60 ms. For learning, `mmap` + `memoryview` is the right cheap primitive.

### 4. Ranking: BM25 and beyond

**(a) Mechanism.** BM25 (Robertson et al., "Okapi at TREC-3", 1994), from the probabilistic relevance framework:

```
score(d,Q) = Σ_{t∈Q} IDF(t) · ( f(t,d)·(k1+1) ) / ( f(t,d) + k1·(1 − b + b·|d|/avgdl) )
```

- `IDF(t) = log(1 + (N − df + 0.5)/(df + 0.5))` — the `+1` keeps IDF non-negative (the **negative-IDF trap**: older `log((N−df+0.5)/(df+0.5))` goes negative when `df > N/2`).
- `k1` controls **tf saturation**: `k1→∞` near-linear, `k1=0` ignores tf entirely (pure IDF presence).
- `b` controls **length normalization**: `b=0` disables, `b=1` full. Default 0.75.
  Top-`k` uses a **bounded min-heap of size k**: O(n log k), never a full sort.

**(b) Production.** Defaults `k1=1.2, b=0.75, discountOverlaps=true`. Lucene's IDF is exactly `log(1 + (docCount − docFreq + 0.5)/(docFreq + 0.5))`. **Quantized norm byte**: `computeNorm` stores field length in one byte via `SmallFloat.intToByte4` — a float-like encoding keeping ~4 significant bits (3 explicit mantissa bits plus an implicit leading bit, plus an exponent), with small lengths (below `NUM_FREE_VALUES`) stored exactly. The consequence: many distinct lengths collapse to the same of 256 norm values; the Javadoc guarantees only *monotonicity* (`Long.compareUnsigned(n1,n2)>0 ⟹ score(freq,n1) ≤ score(freq,n2)`), so two documents of length 1000 and 1024 can get identical length normalization. In exchange, Lucene precomputes a **256-entry `float[]` cache/`normTable`** per query term: `cache[i] = k1·((1−b) + b·decodeNorm(i)/avgdl)`, so per-document scoring is a single array lookup indexed by the norm byte. **Steal this exactly.** (Historical note: pre-7.0 Lucene stored `boost/sqrt(length)` via `floatToByte315`, a classic 8-bit float with a 3-bit mantissa and 5-bit exponent; 7.0+ stores the raw length via `intToByte4` and moved the length-norm math into the query-time table.) Variants: **BM25F** (combine per-field tf with weights *before* saturation, not summed BM25 scores); **BM25+/BM25L** add a lower-bound shift δ (Lucene BM25L: `k1=1.25, b=0.4, d=0.5`).

**Dynamic pruning:** **MAXSCORE** (Turtle & Flood — partition query terms into essential/non-essential vs the current heap threshold, skip docs with only non-essential terms); **WAND** (Broder et al. — per-term max upper bound + pivot skip; reduced full evaluations by >90% on TREC Web data "almost without any loss in precision or recall"); **Block-Max WAND** (Ding & Suel 2011 — max score per 128-block; LUCENE-8135, shipped Lucene 8, 2019). Elastic's measured results (jpountz): recording impacts + block-max WAND made **term queries between 3× and 7× faster, conjunctions between 3% and 7× faster, and disjunctions between −8% and 15× faster** on Lucene's benchmark suite; a follow-on exploiting BM25's natural score bound made **disjunctions between 40% and 13× faster**. Lucene later "got attracted to the lower overhead of MAXSCORE and started using block-max MAXSCORE for top-level disjunctions in **July 2022** (annotation EN in Lucene's nightly benchmarks)." Lucene stores per-block **"impacts"** — pareto-optimal `(term-frequency, norm)` tuples — in a skip-list-parallel structure, so it computes a block's max possible score without decoding it; tail blocks (<128 docs) use a `DUMMY_IMPACTS = Impact(Integer.MAX_VALUE, 1L)` placeholder. BM25 stops being enough on vocabulary mismatch ("car" vs "automobile") — see §11.

**(c) Frontier.** Block-Max Pruning (SIGIR 2024) and Dynamic Superblock Pruning (2025). Implement BM25 + byte-norm table + heap first; add WAND once proven against a brute-force oracle.

### 5. Near-real-time indexing: buffers, refresh, durability

**(a) Mechanism.** New docs accumulate in an **in-memory buffer** (not searchable). A **refresh** closes the buffer, writes a new immutable segment, opens it for search — the NRT contract. A **flush/commit** `fsync`s and writes a commit point (durability). Three distinct ops: **refresh = visibility, flush = durability, commit = recoverable on-disk point.** A **translog/WAL** appends every write so it can replay after a crash, decoupling durability from segment commit.

**(b) Production.** `index.refresh_interval` defaults to **1 second** (origin of "near-real-time"); each refresh creates a segment. Setting 200–500ms to "make search faster" **backfires** (more segments, more merges). `refresh_interval: -1` disables it — forget to re-enable and the index goes silent to search. `?refresh=true` per write destroys throughput. Elastic's own "Tune for indexing speed" guidance is to raise `index.refresh_interval` "to a larger value, e.g. 30s" during heavy indexing; raising it from 1s to 30s during bulk loads can **increase throughput by 30–50% on write-bound clusters**. Durability is separate: translog `fsync`s per request by default (`durability: request`); `async` fsyncs every `sync_interval` (default 5s), risking up to 5s loss. Flush is triggered by translog size (default `flush_threshold_size: 512mb`).

**(c) Frontier.** Stateless/object-store makes the WAL the durable primary; segment upload is async; "refresh" = publish to metastore. Quickwit commits every ~10s (configurable to 5s). For learning: buffer → refresh → immutable segment, a manual `refresh` endpoint, optional append-only WAL.

### 6. Merging, deletes, and updates

**(a) Mechanism.** Many small segments mean every query searches every segment, so query cost grows with segment count. A **merge policy** compacts N segments into one larger immutable segment and retires inputs — **LSM compaction in search clothing**. Deletes are **tombstones**: a `LiveDocs` bitmap consulted at query time; space reclaimed only when a merge rewrites the segment without dead docs. An update is **delete + re-insert**.

**(b) Production.** Lucene's **`TieredMergePolicy`** defaults: `maxMergeAtOnce`=10, `maxMergedSegmentMB`=5 GB (segments above are left alone), `floorSegmentMB`=2 MB (tiny segments rounded up to prevent a long tail), `deletesPctAllowed`=33% (range 20–50), `forceMergeDeletesPctAllowed`=10%. **Write amplification**: each doc rewritten several times climbing tiers — the budgeted cost of low segment count. Merges are I/O-throttled. **`force_merge` is a footgun**: great on a static index, disastrous on an actively-written one — it makes one huge segment that can't merge normally, accumulates unreclaimable deletes, and is enormous to recover. From Lucene 7.5, if `maxNumSegments` conflicts with `maxMergedSegmentMB`, `maxNumSegments` wins, so force-merge deliberately breaks the size cap. Tombstone persistence: `LiveDocs` must be persisted (new deletes-file generation) or a crash resurrects deleted docs. Version conflicts use optimistic concurrency (`_version`/seq numbers). **Segment count → latency**: each extra segment is another dictionary lookup and postings iterator to merge — roughly linear per-query fixed overhead, hence the "≤20 segments/shard" target.

**(c) Frontier.** Object-store merges run on separate indexing compute; the merged split is uploaded. For learning: a simple tiered policy (merge when ≥K similar-size segments), a `LiveDocs` roaring bitmap, reclaim on merge, a merge-count metric. **Skip force-merge** except as an admin op on a quiesced index.

### 7. Sharding, scatter-gather, distributed scoring

**(a) Mechanism.** The corpus is partitioned across N independent local indexes (**shards**). A search **fans out** concurrently, each returns its **local top-`k`**, the coordinator merges into a **global top-`k`**. Correctness: the global top-`k` is a subset of the union of per-shard top-`k`s, so gathering `k` from each and merging is exact — *provided scores are comparable*. The catch: **BM25 IDF uses per-shard stats** (`N`, `df`), so scores are **not strictly comparable across shards**.

**(b) Production.** Routing: `hash(routing_key) mod num_primary_shards` — why `num_shards` is fixed at creation. **Query-then-fetch** (default): phase 1 shards return top-`k` IDs+scores from local stats; phase 2 coordinator fetches docs for the global top-`k`. **DFS-query-then-fetch** adds a preliminary round for *global* term stats, so every shard scores with the same `N`/`df` — more accurate, extra round trip; the skew is worst for rare terms on small/uneven shards. **Shard sizing**: 10–50 GB, under ~200M docs/shard (Lucene hard cap ~2 billion but latency degrades far earlier); the "20 shards/GB heap" rule was **deprecated in ES 8.3** (now the size range + `cluster.max_shards_per_node` default 1000); heap = `min(50% RAM, 31 GB)` (above 31 GB loses compressed oops), other 50% for page cache.

**Tail latency** dominates fan-out. Dean & Barroso, "The Tail at Scale" (CACM 2013): each backend p99=10ms, fan-out to 100 → end-to-end p99 near 140ms; a 1-in-100 slow backend delays ~63% of requests. Their headline BigTable benchmark reads 1,000 keys across 100 servers; a hedging request sent after a 10ms delay cut the 99.9th-percentile latency **from 1,800ms to 74ms while sending just 2% more requests**. Other tail-tolerant fixes: **tied requests** (enqueue on two replicas that cancel each other on start), **per-shard timeouts with partial results**, **adaptive replica selection** (C3). Deep pagination: `from+size` deep paging forces every shard to return `from+size` (O(N·(from+size)) memory); fixes are **`search_after`** (cursor) and **PIT/scroll** (frozen view).

**(c) Frontier.** Stateless search lets any node answer any query. For learning on one machine: shards as N independent local indexes searched concurrently; gather top-`k`-per-shard; **compute a global `df` cheaply** (you have all shards!) to sidestep IDF skew — a win a distributed system pays a DFS round-trip for. Add per-shard timeouts with partial results.

### 8. Caching a search engine

**(a) Mechanism.** Cacheable layers: whole **query results**; **filter bitsets**; **doc values**; the **page cache** itself. Any cached result must be invalidated on refresh/merge/delete.

**(b) Production.** **Shard request cache**: whole-shard result keyed by a hash of the request body, **invalidated whenever the shard refreshes** — tied to exact segment state, so "you always get the same result as an uncached search." On a hot index refreshing every 1s, entries barely live; on a rarely-refreshed index, hit rates **exceed 90%**. Queries using `now` aren't cached unless rounded. **Node query cache**: **filter bitsets per segment** — because segments are immutable, adding a new segment *doesn't* invalidate existing entries. Lesson: **cache at the granularity of an immutable unit (the segment) and invalidation is trivial.** **Single-flight / request coalescing**: when many concurrent requests miss the same hot key, exactly one computes and the rest await it — avoids a **cache stampede** (thundering herd right after a refresh invalidates a hot key). Fix: a per-key `dict[key, asyncio.Future]`. **Zipfian traffic** is why caching works: a small hot set recurs while a long tail is nearly unique; 80%+ hit ratio is achievable because the head is small and hot; the tail still benefits from the shared page cache.

**(c) Frontier.** Object-store search caches index metadata and hot byte ranges locally. For learning: a bounded LRU results cache keyed by `(analyzed_query, k, set_of_live_segment_ids)`, invalidated structurally on segment change; single-flight via `asyncio.Future`; a hit-ratio metric.

### 9. Concurrency, runtime, and the CPython ceiling

**(a) Mechanism.** Immutable segments give **lock-free reads** — writers only create new segments, never mutate. You must hand a searcher a **consistent point-in-time view** (fixed segments + `LiveDocs`). Lucene uses `SearcherManager` + **reference counting**: a searcher references the current `IndexReader`; a refresh opens a new one; old readers close only at refcount zero. Writes are serialized (one `IndexWriter`).

**(b) CPython reality.** The user wants to know *which mechanism is the ceiling*.

- **The GIL** serializes Python bytecode. The BM25 scoring loop is **CPU-bound Python**, so in the standard build N threads scoring N shards run *serially* — this, not segment count or I/O, is the throughput ceiling.
- **Free-threaded CPython (3.13 `--disable-gil`, PEP 703)**: ~**2.2× on 4 CPU-bound threads (3.13t), ~3.09× (3.14t)**. The single-thread penalty **dropped from ~40% in 3.13t to roughly 5–10% in 3.14t**; free threading was experimental in 3.13 and became officially supported via **PEP 779 (released October 2025)** in 3.14. Most promising path — benchmark it against your own scoring loop.
- **Process pool**: sidesteps the GIL but **pickling postings is expensive** — have each process *own* its shard's mmap'd segments (shared via page cache) and return only the small top-`k`. Process-per-shard is the pragmatic pre-3.14 answer.
- **Subinterpreters** (PEP 734): lighter than processes but data-sharing isn't solved; often slower.
- **GC pauses**: a million postings as small Python objects invites long pauses and huge overhead — keep postings as packed bytes in `array`/`struct`/NumPy via `memoryview`. `__slots__` removes per-instance `__dict__`; `gc.freeze()` after loading static data removes it from the scan set; `gc.disable()` in a latency-critical section (re-enable after).
- **Allocation churn**: allocating per posting per query is death — preallocate the heap, reuse buffers, score into arrays.
- **Blocking FS on the loop**: an `open()/read()`/page-fault on the loop thread stalls all requests. Detect via `PYTHONASYNCIODEBUG=1`; fix with `asyncio.to_thread` or resident segments.
- **C/Rust extensions** (PyO3, Cython): SIMD, GIL released around the native loop, no per-integer Python overhead — 1–2 orders of magnitude on scoring. Tantivy (Rust) *is* this.

**Where CPython cannot reach:** at ≥2000 searches/sec, p99 ≤ 50ms over 1M docs, the standard-build GIL serializes scoring and becomes the ceiling before segment count or mmap. Escape via (a) free-threaded 3.14 + per-shard threads, (b) process-per-shard, or (c) a native scoring extension. The **cache (≥80% hit ratio on Zipfian) is what makes 2000 req/s survivable** — 80% of requests never touch the scoring loop.

**(c) Frontier.** Free-threaded CPython maturing through 3.14/3.15; the JIT is orthogonal and doesn't yet help this workload much. Build the scoring loop over `memoryview`/NumPy; keep shards independent so you can flip thread-pool vs process-pool.

### 10. Benchmarking and correctness

**(a) Mechanism.** Two questions: **correct** (right docs, right order) and **fast** (latency/throughput). Different tools.
**Correctness:** a **brute-force reference** (linear scan, score every doc, full sort) is your **oracle** — dynamic pruning is rank-safe, so results must match exactly (catches heap bugs, skip errors, unsafe pruning). **Property-based tests** (Hypothesis): analysis idempotence, a merge **preserves live postings**, delete hides docs, index+refresh makes a doc findable. **Fuzzing** the on-disk format: truncate, flip bytes, assert an error, *never* wrong postings — where the CRC footer earns its keep.
**Performance:** relevance = **BEIR** (zero-shot, 18 datasets), **MS MARCO**, **TREC** (nDCG/MRR/recall — quality, not speed); load = **`wrk2`, `k6`, `oha`, Vegeta**; Elasticsearch has **Rally** (`esrally`); Lucene runs **nightly benchmarks** (luceneutil).

**(b) Coordinated omission.** The key lesson: **use an open-model (constant-arrival-rate) load generator**. Coordinated omission (Gil Tene): a closed-loop tester sends the next request only after the previous returns, so during a stall it stops sending — the piled-up requests are never measured and reported p99 is a fantasy. Real symptom: a test reports p99=47ms; production shows 1.8s — a 38× gap of pure measurement error. `wrk2` fires at a constant rate and measures latency against *intended* send time (`t_response − t_intended`), recording into an **HdrHistogram**; use ≥5000 samples for a stable p99. Corpus: replay a **Zipfian** query mix (small hot set + unique tail) to test the ≥80% cache target — uniform-random measures the wrong hit ratio.

**(c) Frontier.** LLM-judged relevance and newer BEIR suites. For learning: write the brute-force oracle first (~30 lines), property-test merge/delete, fuzz the format, load-test with `wrk2`/`oha` on a Zipfian replay + HdrHistogram.

### 11. Advanced & emerging topics (2025–2026)

**Learned sparse retrieval (SPLADE, uniCOIL).** A transformer predicts term *impact weights* and expands docs, but stores them **in an ordinary inverted index** — reuse your postings machinery with float impacts instead of tf. Wins: neural relevance with inverted-index efficiency, addresses vocabulary mismatch. Maturity: production-viable, but its score distributions (longer queries, smaller vocab, flatter curves) broke classic WAND — motivating BMP. Borrowable? Retrieval side yes; the model needs a GPU, so precompute impacts offline.
**Dense vectors + HNSW/DiskANN.** Embeddings + ANN. HNSW dominates in-memory; DiskANN targets SSD. Wins: semantic match. Cost: memory, no exact lexical match, build cost. A toy HNSW is a distraction from the inverted-index goal; skip unless hybrid is the point.
**Hybrid + Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF).** Fuse rankers by **rank position, discarding raw scores**: `RRF(d) = Σ 1/(k + rank_r(d))`, `k=60` default (Cormack, Clarke & Büttcher, "Reciprocal Rank Fusion outperforms Condorcet and individual rank learning methods," SIGIR '09, which showed RRF "consistently yields better results than any individual system, and better results than the standard method Condorcet Fuse"). `k=60` makes rank 1 vs 2 a small difference (rewarding *consensus* over any single #1) while the tail still contributes. Wins: sidesteps BM25-vs-cosine incompatibility with zero normalization; used under the hood by Elasticsearch's `rrf` retriever, OpenSearch, Weaviate, Qdrant and Azure AI Search. **The cheapest frontier idea to steal** — ~10 lines, no training.
**Late interaction (ColBERT/PLAID).** Per-token embeddings + "MaxSim" at query time. Wins: better relevance than single-vector dense. Cost: storage explodes (a vector per token), expensive queries; PLAID/Seismic (2024–2025) attack that. Research-to-early-production; not cheap at learning scale.
**Segments on object storage / stateless search.** Quickwit stores immutable "splits" on S3; **stateless searchers** fetch only needed byte ranges and are disposable (any node answers any query). Built on **tantivy** (Rust). Decouples compute from storage (like Datadog's Husky), separates indexing from search, opens a split on S3 in ~60ms via hotcache. Elasticsearch has **stateless** work; **Turbopuffer** and **S3 Express One Zone** push the same. Wins: ~10× cheaper storage, independent scaling, no data loss on node failure. Merge moves to indexing compute; refresh = publish to metastore; commit cadence relaxes (~10s vs 1s). Production for observability (low-QPS, high-volume). The *concept* illuminates; a single machine doesn't need it — mmap over local disk teaches the same immutability lesson.
**Block-Max Pruning (BMP) + Dynamic Superblock Pruning.** BMP (Mallia, Suel, Tonellotto, SIGIR 2024, PISA engine) targets learned-sparse indexes: partition doc space into small consecutive blocks, aggregate/sort on the fly, process only what safe early termination requires; substantially outperforms WAND on SPLADE, needs impacts quantized to 8 bits. 2025 follow-ons: Dynamic Superblock Pruning, Seismic (k-means-clustered blocks). Research with reference Rust/C++ in PISA. The *idea* (block max scores) is what you'd already build for Block-Max WAND.
**SIMD/GPU decode** (billions of ints/sec; GPUSparse 2026): not reachable in pure CPython. **Tiered/hot-warm storage**: standard in log search; a single machine doesn't need it.
**BM25's stubborn survival.** Across 2025 evaluations BM25 remains the baseline every method must beat and stays competitive (especially out-of-domain, where dense models degrade). **Build BM25 first and well; it's the foundation the frontier is still built on.**

## Recommendations

**Adopt (cheap, high-value):**

1. **Immutable segments + mmap + `memoryview` zero-copy reads** — the spine; gives lock-free reads, trivial caching, NRT refresh nearly free.
2. **A CRC32 footer + magic header on every segment, validated on open** — ~20 lines; turns silent corruption into a clean error. Non-negotiable.
3. **BM25 with quantized byte-norm + a 256-entry precomputed length-norm table.** Copy `k1=1.2, b=0.75` and `log(1 + (N−df+0.5)/(df+0.5))` verbatim; score via a bounded min-heap of size `k`.
4. **Delta + variable-byte postings in 128-doc blocks with skip pointers**, decoded through `memoryview`/NumPy. Stop here — SIMD/PForDelta isn't reachable in CPython and the ratio gain isn't worth a native extension.
5. **A results cache keyed by `(analyzed_query, k, live_segment_set)` with single-flight coalescing** via `asyncio.Future`, invalidated structurally on refresh/merge — makes ≥2000 req/s and ≥80% hit ratio reachable (80% of requests skip scoring).
6. **A brute-force oracle + property tests + format fuzzing** — the only way to trust dynamic pruning.
7. **Open-model load testing (`wrk2`/`oha` + HdrHistogram) on a Zipfian replay** — else coordinated omission makes your p99 fiction.
8. **Compute global `df` across your local shards** — sidestep per-shard IDF skew for free.
9. **Reciprocal Rank Fusion (`k=60`) if you add a second ranker** — ~10 lines, no training.
10. **Structure shards as independent units; plan concurrency now:** free-threaded 3.14 thread-per-shard, or process-per-shard with mmap'd segments (return only top-`k`, never pickle postings). Keep the scoring loop allocation-free (`array`/NumPy, `__slots__`, `gc.freeze()` after load).

**Skip at this scale (essential in production, a distraction here):** DFS-query-then-fetch (you have global stats locally); a translog/WAL (manual refresh + rebuild-from-source suffices unless durability is a goal); automatic `force_merge` (admin-only on a quiesced index — a footgun); hot-warm tiering, searchable snapshots, object-store splits (fascinating, irrelevant on one machine); dense vectors/HNSW/late interaction (a different project); SIMD/GPU decode and FSTs (a sorted block dictionary with front coding beats an FST on clarity-per-line — an FST is a stretch goal, not the 80/20).

**Exotic-but-cheap, steal today:** RRF; Roaring-style bitmap containers for `LiveDocs`/filters (array/bitmap/run, ≤8 KB per chunk); Block-Max upper bounds per 128-doc block (one max score per block; enables WAND for near-free once postings are blocked).

**Thresholds that change decisions:**

- **Live segments/shard above ~20** → merge policy too lazy; lower the trigger.
- **p99 > 50ms with CPU pegged in scoring** → the GIL is the ceiling; move to free-threaded 3.14 or process-per-shard before touching the algorithm.
- **Cache hit ratio below ~80% under Zipfian load** → invalidation too aggressive (dropping the whole cache per refresh) or cache undersized; move to per-segment caching.
- **GC pause time in p99** → postings are Python objects; move to packed buffers + `gc.freeze()`.
- **Oracle and engine disagree on top-`k`** → dynamic pruning isn't rank-safe; disable until they match.

## Caveats

- **Version-dependence is everywhere.** Lucene's norm encoding changed at 7.0 (`floatToByte315` storing `boost/sqrt(len)` → `intToByte4` storing raw length); the default top-level disjunction strategy changed to block-max MAXSCORE (July 2022); the `MMapDirectory` Panama migration spans JDK 19 (preview) to 22 (supported). ES's "20 shards/GB heap" rule was deprecated in 8.3. Cite the version you target.
- **Free-threaded CPython numbers are early and workload-specific.** The ~2.2×/3.09× speedups and the ~40%→5–10% single-thread-penalty figures come from general suites (pyperformance, Fibonacci-style microbenchmarks), not a search scoring loop — measure your own. Experimental in 3.13, officially supported in 3.14 via PEP 779 (October 2025).
- **Published performance numbers came from other hardware/workloads.** "Billions of ints/sec" assumes SIMD on specific Intel CPUs; TurboPFor's ">8 GB/s" is C+AVX2; Lucene's "3×–13×" figures are from its own JVM benchmark suite. Order-of-magnitude guidance, not CPython targets.
- **The hedged-request numbers (1,800ms→74ms)** are from Google's 2013 BigTable benchmark at 100+ backends; the mechanism transfers, the magnitude does not.
- **Vendor-authored benchmarks** (Elastic blogs by the Lucene committer who wrote the feature; Quickwit's "10× cheaper") are credible but self-interested — trust the directions, not the exact multipliers.
- **Per-shard IDF skew magnitude "in practice"** is asserted qualitatively across sources (worst for rare terms / small shards); I found no single primary quantification of how many ranking positions it typically moves — treat "usually negligible on large even shards" as received wisdom.
- **Some source-code details** (SmallFloat bit layout, the BM25Similarity 256-entry cache) were verified against third-party Lucene source mirrors consistent with the official Javadoc rather than always a pinned apache/lucene commit; descriptions match across mirrors but line-exact code varies by version.
